# ERQ — plain-language guide

**What it measures:** Emotion regulation (Emotion Regulation)

**Who it's for:** Adults · **How it's given:** Self-report

**Length:** 10 items · **Scores from 1 to 7

## What this test is

ERQ (Emotion Regulation Questionnaire) is a self-report questionnaire that measures **emotion regulation**. This instrument is well validated in the research literature.

## What the questions look like

Each item is answered on one scale: Strongly disagree (1), Disagree (2), Somewhat disagree (3), Neither agree nor disagree (4), Somewhat agree (5), Agree (6), Strongly agree (7).

<details><summary>Example items (10 total)</summary>

- When I want to feel more positive emotion (such as joy or amusement), I change what I'm thinking about.
- I keep my emotions to myself.
- When I want to feel less negative emotion (such as sadness or anger), I change what I'm thinking about.
- When I am feeling positive emotions, I am careful not to express them.
- When I'm faced with a stressful situation, I make myself think about it in a way that helps me stay calm.

## How it's scored

**Method:** mean. Two independent subscale means (do NOT sum the 10 items into a total): Cognitive Reappraisal (6 items) and Expressive Suppression (4 items). Original validation (Gross & John 2003): alphas reappraisal = .88, suppression = .85. Publication norm: report each subscale mean (1-7); no total score, no clinical cutoffs — interpret relative to sample norms (e.g., US college mean reappraisal ≈ 4.6, suppression ≈ 3.0).

### Subscales

- **Cognitive Reappraisal** — items 1, 3, 5, 7, 9, 10
- **Expressive Suppression** — items 2, 4, 6, 8

## What the scores mean

There is **no official cutoff** — this instrument produces a continuous score that is interpreted relative to comparison groups rather than a pass/fail threshold.

## Usage & licensing

**Free** — Widely used for research with attribution; James Gross permits research use without fee.
